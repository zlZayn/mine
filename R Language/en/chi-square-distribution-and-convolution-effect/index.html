<p>Many people have heard: <strong>The higher the degrees of freedom of a chi-square distribution, the closer it approximates a normal distribution (central limit theorem)</strong>. But why exactly? And what is a chi-square distribution anyway?</p> <h2>Basic Definition</h2> <p>Chi-square distribution,</p> <ul> <li><strong>Definition</strong>: If there are $k$ <strong>independent, standard normal random variables</strong> ($Z_1, Z_2, ..., Z_k$), then the <strong>sum of their squares</strong> follows a chi-square distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom ($df=k$). Mathematically:</li> </ul> <p>$$X = {Z_1}^2 + {Z_2}^2 + ... + {Z_k}^2$$</p> <p>Here, $X$ follows a chi-square distribution ( $X \sim \chi^2(k)$ ), and $k$ is its degrees of freedom.</p> <p>Since every term in a chi-square distribution is a square, all values are greater than 0.</p> <h2>Initial Observations</h2> <p>This graph is quite familiar: (Simulated with one million data points)</p> <p><img alt="" src="https://pic1.zhimg.com/v2-322524d83c27b059bcc71db0afb81eee_1440w.jpg"></p> <p>For $df=1$, it's easy to understand—it's simply the square of a standard normal distribution $N(0,1)$, so the density is highest near 0.</p> <p>For $df=2$, $X = {Z_1}^2 + {Z_2}^2$ is the sum of squares of two standard normal distributions. <strong>Note that these two are independent!</strong> The highest density is still near 0.</p> <p>For $df=3$, $X = {Z_1}^2 + {Z_2}^2 + {Z_3}^2$ is the sum of squares of three standard normal distributions. For $X$ to be 0, all three terms must be 0. Due to the <strong>smoothing effect of convolution</strong>, this probability becomes smaller, and this is already noticeable.</p> <p>And so on. <strong>As $df$ increases, there are more terms, and the smoothing effect of convolution becomes more pronounced.</strong></p> <p>We can understand the smoothing effect of convolution this way: When degrees of freedom exceed 2, for the sum of squares to be close to 0, all standard normal variables must be close to 0 <strong>simultaneously</strong>. While the probability of each individual variable being close to 0 is not low, the probability of multiple variables being close to 0 simultaneously decreases as the number of variables increases. Moreover, as degrees of freedom increase, the probability of the sum of squares taking small values becomes increasingly smaller, because as long as one variable is not close to 0, the sum of squares will become larger.</p> <p>A simple example that helps understand the smoothing effect of convolution is:</p> <h2>Rolling Dice (Insert)</h2> <p>Convolution is a mathematical operation that calculates "all possible combinations" and is used in probability theory to compute the distribution of the sum of two independent random variables.</p> <p>For easier understanding, rolling dice is an example of <strong>discrete convolution</strong>.</p> <ul> <li>Single roll: Uniform distribution (1-6 points, each with probability $\frac{1}{6}$)</li> <li> <p>Sum of two rolls: Triangular distribution (7 points have the highest probability):</p> </li> <li> <p>Sum of two rolls being 2 (extreme value): Requires both rolls to be 1, with probability $\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^2$</p> </li> <li>Sum of two rolls being 12 (extreme value): Requires both rolls to be 6, also with probability $\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^2$</li> <li>Sum of two rolls being 7 (middle value): Can be achieved in many ways—(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)—six different combinations</li> </ul> <p><img alt="" src="https://pica.zhimg.com/v2-ec2c9755590e7db249cd54d8b104894c_1440w.jpg"></p> <ul> <li>Sum of three rolls: Begins to show a bell shape</li> </ul> <p><img alt="" src="https://pic3.zhimg.com/v2-23959f2b1f33a0c0ceee1b0ff213d94c_1440w.jpg"></p> <ul> <li>Sum of many rolls: Approaches a normal distribution</li> </ul> <p><img alt="" src="https://pica.zhimg.com/v2-f0f60c2d8b9318cca3fe2c8325a55636_1440w.jpg"></p> <p><strong>This is the smoothing effect of convolution: Middle values have more combinations, so they have higher probabilities!</strong></p> <h2>Direct Visualization</h2> <p>The chi-square distribution involves continuous convolution.</p> <p>Let's continue with the intuitive observation.</p> <p><strong>As $df$ increases, there are more terms, and the smoothing effect of convolution becomes more pronounced.</strong></p> <p><img alt="" src="https://pic2.zhimg.com/v2-c0b6b6f079e0748d91f968e84c013a77_1440w.jpg"></p> <p>When $df$ is small, the distribution is right-skewed. As $df$ increases, it becomes more and more like a normal distribution. Notice the peak's position on the x-axis—it gradually approaches $df$ (at the red line) from left to right.</p> <h2>Mathematical Derivation</h2> <p>What is the value on the x-axis corresponding to the peak? What is its relationship with $df$? Why does it happen when $df &gt; 2$?</p> <h3>1. Probability Density Function of Chi-square Distribution</h3> <p><strong>The probability density function (PDF) of a chi-square distribution with $k$ degrees of freedom is:</strong></p> <p>$$f(x;k) = \frac{1}{2^{\frac{k}{2}} \Gamma\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)}\cdot x^{\frac{k}{2}-1}\cdot e^{-\frac{x}{2}}, \quad x &gt; 0$$</p> <h3>2. Taking Logarithm to Simplify Differentiation</h3> <p>$$\ln f(x;k) = \ln\left[\frac{1}{2^{\frac{k}{2}} \Gamma\left( \frac{k}{2} \right)} \right] + \ln x^{\frac{k}{2} - 1} + \ln e^{-\frac{x}{2}} $$</p> <p>$$\ln f(x;k) = C + \left(\frac{k}{2} - 1\right) \ln x - \frac{x}{2}$$</p> <p>Where $C = \ln\left[\frac{1}{2^{\frac{k}{2}} \Gamma\left( \frac{k}{2} \right)} \right]$ is a constant.</p> <h3>3. Differentiating with respect to $x$ and Setting Derivative to Zero</h3> <p>$$\frac{d}{dx} [\ln f(x;k)] = \frac{\frac{k}{2} - 1}{x} - \frac{1}{2}$$</p> <p>Setting the derivative to zero:</p> <p>$$\frac{\frac{k}{2} - 1}{x} - \frac{1}{2} = 0$$</p> <p>$$x = k - 2$$</p> <p>To verify it's a maximum point, compute the second derivative:</p> <p>$$\frac{d^2}{dx^2} [\ln f(x;k)] = -\frac{\frac{k}{2} - 1}{x^2}$$</p> <p>When $k &gt; 2$, the second derivative is negative, confirming $x = k - 2$ is a maximum point.</p> <h3>4. Function Behavior Decomposition Method</h3> <p>Returning to the original PDF, we can derive the <strong>proportional form of the PDF</strong>:</p> <p>$$f(x;k) \propto x^{\frac{k}{2}-1} \cdot e^{-\frac{x}{2}}, \quad x &gt; 0$$</p> <table> <thead> <tr> <th>Degrees of Freedom</th> <th>Key Feature</th> <th>Mode</th> </tr> </thead> <tbody> <tr> <td>df=1</td> <td>Singularity at x=0 (approaching infinity)</td> <td>0</td> </tr> <tr> <td>df=2</td> <td>Maximum at x=0, degenerates to pure exponential function</td> <td>0</td> </tr> <tr> <td>df=3</td> <td>Exponent becomes positive for the first time! Polynomial term dominates (grows) when x is small</td> <td>1</td> </tr> <tr> <td>df=4</td> <td>Polynomial term dominates (grows) when x is small</td> <td>2</td> </tr> </tbody> </table> <p>Due to the exponential nature of $e^{-\frac{x}{2}}$, it will always dominate when x is sufficiently large (ensuring the right side always decays), and together with the polynomial term, they produce the mode.</p> <h3>5. Conclusion</h3> <p>For $k &gt; 2$, the mode of the chi-square distribution (the x-value corresponding to the peak of its density plot) is $k - 2$; for $0 &lt; k \leq 2$, the mode is 0. Expressed piecewise:</p> <p>$$\text{Mode}(k) = \begin{cases} 0, &amp; 0 &lt; k \leq 2 \ k - 2, &amp; k &gt; 2 \end{cases}$$</p> <p><strong>As $df$ (i.e., $k$) increases, the mode gets closer to $df$:</strong></p> <p><img alt="" src="https://pic1.zhimg.com/v2-51bb23fe499edb6253c2569b63cbd24c_1440w.jpg"><img alt="" src="https://pic4.zhimg.com/v2-c7ec2ec9344dce86abccd7556b0107fd_1440w.jpg"><img alt="" src="https://pic4.zhimg.com/v2-8f28429d70c9767a511a011ce84223dd_1440w.jpg"></p> <p>The larger $df$ (i.e., $k$ in the formula), the closer they get, because when $k$ is large enough, we can consider $k-2=k$, and the gap is always 2!</p> <p>Don't forget! $k-2$ is the x-value corresponding to the peak, and $k$ is the $df$.</p> <h2>Final Points</h2> <ul> <li><strong>A chi-square distribution is formed by the sum (convolution) of squares of independent standard normal random variables. Its morphological evolution perfectly demonstrates the smoothing effect of convolution.</strong></li> <li><strong>The smoothing effect of convolution: Middle values have more combinations, so they
